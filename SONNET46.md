# The Sell-Off is the Signal

**The AI buildout doesn't care about the Iran premium. It doesn't care about oil at $100. And it certainly doesn't care that software investors panicked in February.**

Here's what we know as of March 15, 2026: markets are down 5–8% from their February peaks. A war premium has embedded itself in energy prices. Enterprise software has been taken out behind the shed and shot — many names now sitting 25–40% below all-time highs on fears that AI will displace the very software it's being built on top of. The mood is cautious. The headlines are loud.

And somewhere in Veldhoven, Netherlands, ASML's order book is booked out for years. Somewhere in Santa Clara, Applied Materials just guided 20%+ equipment sales growth for 2026. Somewhere in a KLA fab, inspection tools are running 24 hours a day because the chipmakers of the world cannot afford yield losses at 2nm.

The machines don't stop because oil spiked.

---

## The Gold Rush Frame

Every great capital cycle in history had two kinds of winners. The miners — the people who found the gold — and the people who sold them shovels, pickaxes, and denim jeans. The gold rush gets the headlines. The shovel-sellers get the compounding returns.

We are inside the largest capital expenditure cycle in human history. Barclays now estimates the AI infrastructure buildout is nowhere near its peak. Meta committed over $60 billion in AI capex for 2026 alone. Microsoft's remaining performance obligations — revenue already contracted and locked in — reached $625 billion last quarter. Oracle's OCI cloud grew 51% year-over-year and the stock still trades 55% below its all-time high.

This is not a speculative bubble. This is a construction boom with invoices attached to it.

The question isn't whether AI infrastructure spending continues. The question is: who gets paid every time it does?

---

## The Stack, Layer by Layer

We've been mapping this across four layers, and the logic of each one is different.

**Layer One is the foundry equipment complex.** ASML, Applied Materials, KLA, Lam Research. These are the companies that make the machines that make the chips. You cannot build a chip below 7nm without an ASML EUV lithography system. There is no substitute. There is no second supplier. There is one company on earth that makes this machine, it costs upwards of $380 million per unit, and the backlog runs years. That is a structural monopoly, not a cyclical trade. KLA's March 12 Investor Day announced a new $7 billion share repurchase and a 21% dividend raise — the kind of capital return statement that comes from a company that knows, with certainty, what its cash flows look like for the next several years. Lam Research posted record revenue of $20.6 billion in FY2025, up 27% year-over-year, with EPS up 49%. Applied Materials guided 20%+ equipment growth for calendar 2026 and announced co-development partnerships with both SK Hynix and Micron in the same month. The WFE supercycle is not one company talking its own book. It is coordinated, visible, and still in its early chapters.

**Layer Two is design intelligence.** Cadence and Synopsys. Before a chip is fabricated, it must be designed — and every custom AI chip that every hyperscaler is now building runs through EDA software. Google's TPU, Amazon's Trainium, Microsoft's Maia, Meta's MTIA: all of them required Cadence or Synopsys tools to exist. The February software sell-off hit both companies on the thesis that AI would displace enterprise software. Jensen Huang called this exactly wrong. EDA is AI's enabler, not its victim. Cadence CEO Anirudh Devgan said as much explicitly when Q4 FY2025 earnings beat in February. Both stocks remain 23–24% below their all-time highs. That is a pricing error.

**Layer Three is compute, networking, and memory.** Broadcom is co-designing the custom ASICs for Google, Meta, and Apple while its Tomahawk 6 networking switches interconnect the AI clusters those chips will live inside. It joined Goldman's US Conviction List in early March. Arista Networks is the dominant 400G/800G Ethernet switch supplier for hyperscaler AI clusters — its EOS operating system runs the backbone at Microsoft, Meta, and Google simultaneously. SanDisk's Q2 FY2026 results showed 61% year-over-year revenue growth and gross margin expanding from 29.9% to 51.1% in a single quarter — one of the fastest margin expansions in modern semiconductor history. Micron, providing HBM3E for NVIDIA's Blackwell architecture, now sits 34% below its 52-week high while breaking ground on a $100 billion domestic memory complex in New York.

**Layer Four is where investor attention has not fully rotated yet.** Power and physical infrastructure. This is the argument we want to make most forcefully today.

---

## The Binding Constraint

A single NVIDIA GB200 NVL72 rack consumes roughly 120 kilowatts. A hyperscale training cluster of 100,000 GPUs needs gigawatts. The US electrical grid was not designed for this. The constraint on AI scaling in 2026 is not compute. It is not memory. It is not networking.

It is power.

Vertiv — which provides the uninterruptible power supplies, precision cooling, and direct liquid cooling systems inside AI datacenters — joined the S&P 500 in March 2026. It sits 23% below its 52-week high. Constellation Energy, America's largest producer of carbon-free electricity and the primary vehicle for nuclear power purchase agreements with hyperscalers, completed a new 380MW deal with CyrusOne in March. It sits 27% below its October 2025 all-time high. Lumentum received a $2 billion strategic investment from NVIDIA in early March — not a curiosity, but a supply chain co-investment in the optical transceivers that AI infrastructure requires as GPU clusters scale beyond what copper can handle. It also joined the S&P 500 the same month.

The market has not caught up to the power constraint. When it does, the rotation into this layer will be material.

## What the Macro Noise Is Actually Telling You

Three forces are operating simultaneously right now: the Iran/oil shock creating sector rotation toward defense and energy; the February software sell-off creating entry points in enterprise platforms; and the Blackwell ramp creating revenue visibility for the semiconductor complex that is, frankly, rare in any business cycle.

These forces don't cancel each other out. They create terrain.

Oracle is down 55% from its all-time high. Its OCI cloud grew 51% year-over-year, it has $66 billion in remaining performance obligations, and the analyst consensus price target implies 61% upside from current levels. Workday is down 52% from its all-time high on fears that AI replaces HR software — the same logic that would say a railroad is threatened by the goods it carries. Microsoft is 29% below a 52-week high it set while posting $4.14 EPS beats and growing Azure AI at 31% year-over-year. GE Aerospace is 44% below its March 2 all-time high while sitting on a $190 billion backlog — the largest in the company's history.

This is not a market in distress. This is a market in rotation, and rotation creates entry points that won't persist.

---

## The Shovel-Sellers Win Regardless

Here is what grounds this entire thesis: none of the fifteen companies in our AI Infrastructure deep dive — and none of the highest-conviction names in the broader 100-stock universe — depend on any single AI model winning. They don't care if GPT-5 beats Gemini 2.0. They don't care if open-source displaces closed-source. They don't care if the next breakthrough comes from a lab in San Francisco or a university in Shanghai.

ASML gets paid when any chip is manufactured. KLA gets paid when any fab needs to improve yield. Cadence gets paid when any custom silicon needs to be designed. Vertiv gets paid when any datacenter needs to be cooled. Constellation gets paid when any hyperscaler needs reliable baseload power at three in the morning.

These are the toll roads of the AI economy. Toll roads compound. Toll roads don't pick winners.

The sell-off is not a warning. It's an invitation.
