# ### Fundraise snapshot

> **Knowledge base note:** Numbers and metrics here are illustrative from the source; use for methodologies and frameworks, not as current data. For live data use VINCE.

## Content

### Fundraise snapshot
Dragonfly announced a $650 million fourth fund, oversubscribed and capped at that hard limit after an initial $500 million target. The firm described a typical VC first-close/final-close cadence: deploy from a first close, let early winners and follow-on rounds de-risk the vintage, then finish a final close within ~12–18 months; that momentum helped secure the oversubscription. Dragonfly traces its AUM growth from a roughly $100 million first fund (late 2017/early 2018) to a $250 million 2020 fund and a $650 million third fund (final close early 2022), and cited early winners across vintages—names called out include Poly Market, Rain, Athena, Monad, Agora, Pickot and Lighter—as proof points that persuaded new and returning LPs. *"we were overs subscribed. That was the um that was our hard cap."*

### LP dynamics, sizing and returns
LPs pressed on the usual tradeoffs: larger funds make allocation easier for big institutions but carry an inverse correlation risk between AUM and returns; Dragonfly reports higher-than-typical re‑up rates (typical re-up cited in the discussion is around 70–80%, meaning 20–30% churn for many VCs) which helped fundraising. Committees are evaluating more than headline returns: reputational and regulatory exposure (Poly Market was flagged as a sensitive example), compliance, reporting, pedigrees of GP teams, and tangible DPI (distributions to paid-in) all matter, particularly for cautious allocators such as pensions and some family offices. The hosts contrasted strategies that optimize for fee scale versus carried interest, invoked Benchmark’s small‑fund high‑performance model as a counterexample, and named Paradigm and several traditional fintech investors (Ribbit, Lightseed, Norwest) as recurring competitors on deals.

### Token strategy, L1/L2 differences and product-first thesis
The conversation emphasized a product-first rule: tokens become investable when a product has real users, clear utility and organic traction, not when a token is issued prematurely to chase market action. *"If you build a product that people want to use, has real users, has excitement, it does not matter when you launch the token."* Founders are told to "tell me why token"—articulate the design and economic rationale before issuing token supply. The hosts differentiated protocol layers: L1s typically require tokens for decentralized security and long-term incentives, whereas many L2s and application-layer projects can operate without issuing a native token (gas can be ETH or a stablecoin), which helps explain why many L2 tokens have lagged. They discussed Coinbase’s move off the OP Stack and how that engineering/strategic control can precede token plans, highlighted fee capture dynamics (fees can accrue at the L2 rather than flow up to the L1), and reiterated regulatory sensitivity around token launches and insider information; overall the takeaway was that timing and design—not simply “launch a token”—determine whether tokens are attractive investments.
