## Why distribution matters

Key premise: Owning distribution means owning the end user and the economics. If you piggyback on someone else’s distribution (e.g., building for Coinbase or Binance), those platforms control economics and will compress margins. That risks commoditizing your product.

Applications today need direct relationships with users. Founders too often underestimate distribution and over-rely on third-party channels.

## Fat protocol thesis — revisit

The hosts revisit the "fat protocol" thesis (Mangro, Chris Burniske / Placeholder): internet-era protocols were thin, applications captured value; crypto claimed protocols would capture value instead.

Reactions:

- Parts of the thesis still hold (Ethereum, Solana remain major winners and captured value).
- The thesis underestimated how commoditized layer-1s could become and underestimated developer desire for more control and feature sets. Proliferation of chains and vertical integration by apps contradict the simple fat-protocol claim.
- The early L1 winners did gain strong developer network effects (EVM and SVM discussed), but the application era is still early and apps could capture major value over time.

## Aggregation theory / smiling curve

They discuss aggregation theory as a way to understand value capture across supply chains: ends of the supply chain (R&D and user access) tend to capture value, the middle gets squeezed. In crypto today, ecosystems like Ethereum or "Salana" have cultural lock-in; users think of themselves as belonging to an ecosystem. That siloing contributes to distribution power.

## Application-side distribution playbooks (examples)

All winning apps have unique distribution strategies. Key examples discussed:

- Hyperlquid  
  - Native airdrops and token-driven stakeholder wealth created social distribution. Product quality + wealth transfer amplified word-of-mouth.
  - Host view: the airdrop was critical to distribution; hard to replicate the same success.

- Polymarket  
  - Used media distribution: Polymarket statistics were picked up by mainstream media and cited during events (e.g., elections). Non-crypto media exposure drove eyeballs.

- Athena  
  - Built trust and brand among cryptonative users, aligned stakeholders early (Ethereum whales), executed marketing, and partnered with exchanges for distribution.

- FOMO  
  - A social trading app that borrowed product ideas from Web2 (social features, easy onboarding) and leveraged virality.

- Axiom  
  - Executed cashback and referrals well; incentivized community to spread the product.

- Telegram-native projects (e.g., Trojan Banana Gun)  
  - Leveraged Telegram’s viral mechanics: ease of sharing and adding people made distribution straightforward.

Common thread: each app used a different strategy — there’s no one-size-fits-all playbook.

## Generalized distribution frameworks

A few high-level approaches:

- Piggyback on existing networks (borrow the flow): e.g., early Facebook apps, leveraging Google Ads when cheap. Many recent crypto apps piggyback on social channels.
- Own a scarce choke point (the Windows/OS analogy): controlling an operating-layer (wallets, OS-equivalents) creates huge switching costs and distribution advantages.
- Build virality into the product: NFT profile pictures, social features, referral mechanics.
- Aggregate/curate (move one layer up): Google, Spotify are examples of value capture by filtering/curating content or information.
- Token reflexivity as distribution: price appreciation can attract attention and users; done legitimately this can create virtuous cycles, but relying on token price manipulations is brittle and now less palatable.

Practical warning: if you piggyback on platforms like Coinbase or Binance, those platforms control economics and will likely take the margins.

## Infrastructure perspective: market pull vs technology push

Two product-creation modes:

- Market pull: identify a real customer problem, then build the product that solves it.
- Technology push: create a new capability and find markets for it (this is what many L1s did).

Observations:

- Early L1 success (Ethereum) combined technology push with existing latent demand (programmable money).
- Many infra projects assumed durable, defendable moats for L1s; in practice chains are more commoditized and developers seek control and specific developer ergonomics.
- Current successful infra strategies tend to be narrower and opinionated: pick use cases with clear market pull, build tooling and SDKs targeted at those, and expand from a wedge.
- Token price reflexivity remains a distribution channel but must be backed by product/PMF; token-driven distribution without PMF is unsustainable.
- Distribution for infra often looks B2B: solve a pressing problem for builders, get them to adopt, and expand outward.

## New infrastructure problems and opportunities

- Agents, AI, and increases in on-chain volume will create new needs: anti-spam, execution rails, agent access control, and pricing for API/data access.
- Infra that focuses on data, execution, and trust for agents will be compelling.
- Distribution for infra may remain B2B (solve an app/team problem then capture broader adoption).

## AI and agent-native distribution

Thought experiment: if the friction to create products drops toward zero (AI-assisted development), what do moats look like? Distribution becomes even more critical.

Key shifts and recommendations:

- Agents will be a primary consumer: startups must think agent-first, not just human-first.
- Three layers to target for agent-native products:
  1. Data — cheap, contextual, machine-readable data. Agents will prefer the cheapest, fastest, most relevant data sources.
  2. Execution — reliable, policy-aware execution primitives so agents can act on-chain correctly. This includes compliance and policy layers.
  3. Intelligence / evaluation — tools that help agents evaluate other agents, judge trust/risk, and route actions.

- Make docs and SDKs machine-readable; build for models (e.g., "docs for machines") and consider integration into model toolkits.
- Agents are likely to be rational and profit-maximizing. Startups must ask: why would an agent choose my infra?
- Incumbents (OpenAI, Claude, Google Gemini, etc.) have distribution and could capture large parts of the stack. To compete, focus on:
  - Domain-specific agents or compliance-heavy verticals incumbents won't touch.
  - Open, multi-model integrations and interoperability.
  - Superior trust and permissioning layers that incumbents avoid.

Practical suggestions:
- Think where agents "hang out" and how they discover services.
- Focus on speed, cost, and relevance for agent consumption.
- Consider how to be embedded into agent workflows (e.g., a plug-in or tool agents call when executing intents).

## The aggregator risk & brand value

Hosts debate two possible agent futures:

- Agents serve humans (humans retain final control); distribution remains human-influenced.
- Agents serve themselves (profit-maximizing, autonomous); distribution will be based on agent economics.

If aggregator LLMs become dominant interfaces (a "god mode" aggregator), onboarding demand to smaller services becomes harder. Two counterpoints:

- Aggregators are constrained by attention and integration complexity; they can’t solve everything equally well.
- Openness wins: open-source models and open integrations can circumvent closed aggregator dominance (example: community alternatives, competition via open ecosystems).

Brand and human voice remain differentiators. Even if LLMs can generate content, human-brand authenticity and unique hunches create durable value. There is uncertainty whether LLMs will match human voice perfectly — hosts feel current LLM-generated prose often reads "too perfect" and lacks human idiosyncrasies.

## Predictions and meta-observations

- Distribution will be more important than ever in a low-friction product world.
- Lobbying and regulation are likely to increase as product proliferation makes lobbying an attractive moat; crypto policy plays (e.g., Hyperlquid’s policy institute) are seen as strategic investments.
- Open vs closed dynamics: open-source alternatives and decentralized rails may limit incumbent control over agent distribution.
- The "last mile" of AI — making models reliably complete complex tasks end-to-end — remains a crucial unsolved problem and an opportunity for builders.

## Tactical takeaways for founders

- Prioritize distribution early; don’t assume build-it-and-they-will-come.
- Design distribution strategies that cannot be trivially copied or that leverage proprietary access (data, partnerships, regulatory positioning).
- For infra: focus on narrow, high-demand wedges with clear market pull, then broaden.
- For AI/agent-native products: build for machines (docs, SDKs), think about speed/cost/relevance, and identify why an agent would choose your service.
- Brand and human authenticity still matter for sustained attention and trust.

## Closing

Hosts wrap up with plans to meet in person at upcoming events and a final note: distribution strategies are changing rapidly with AI and agentic systems. Builders should adapt to agent-native paradigms and keep distribution front-and-center.
