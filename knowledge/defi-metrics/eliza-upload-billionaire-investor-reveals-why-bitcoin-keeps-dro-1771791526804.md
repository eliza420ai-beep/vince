## Intro: Bitcoin had a rough year
Mike: Listen, Bitcoin had a horrible year. Why do I think it happened? We had a $9 billion seller. I had a friend that wanted to buy a sports team, so he sold some Bitcoin to buy a sports team or a boat — everyone took a few chips off the table. I think we put a low in at 60. I'm not 100% convinced, but I'm pretty convinced. So, yes, I've been buying some Bitcoin recently.

I think one of the reasons why crypto's not going away is that it's now part of everyone's dashboard and institutional infrastructure. People are dabbling, although a lot of the old guys don't have a lot of dry powder.

## The investment environment is more complicated now
Interviewer: You've been in the macro world for decades. What's going on?

Mike: It's complicated. The last 10 years of investing felt easier — everything was generally up. Now it feels much more macro-oriented and harder to preserve and grow value. There's so much changing that it's more difficult to know where to put capital just to stay level.

If you measured yourself in gold, you didn't have a good year last year unless you owned a lot of gold. Does that mean run into gold now? I don't think so.

## Gold and silver cycles
Mike: Gold and silver both put in big moves. Silver put in a cycle high. If you take the silver chart and overlay it with other historical bubbles — the NASDAQ bubble, the Nikkei bubble in '89, microstrategy — you'll see similarities. You might buy silver tactically because it gets oversold and bounces, but I would bet my clothes that silver putting in a new high next year is unlikely. It was an amazing story last year, though.

## The US and the reserve currency
Mike: I think the United States is losing its position as the reserve currency of the world. You can see it by the amount of Treasuries held by foreign central banks — they're going straight down. Part of this is by design and political posture: this administration has been tougher with allies, which has led some countries to diversify their assets and partner more with China.

But China is nowhere close to being a reserve currency because it has a closed current account and no convertible currency. I asked several Chinese billionaires how soon China would open up a convertible currency; the most optimistic prediction was 20 years. Currency is ultimately about trust — trust that the people in charge will be fair with your money.

Europe isn't the answer either. When I graduated in 1987, European and U.S. GDP was roughly the same; today the U.S. is almost double. Europe has a lot of good things, but it's not going to be the reserve currency.

So we may be headed to a world without a clear single reserve currency.

## Assets, inflation, and AI
Mike: The idea I had is a "global wealth currency" — essentially an S&P-style index of the biggest 500 companies in the world. As countries debate and debase their currencies due to debt problems, asset prices go up.

What we've learned (and what Kevin Warsh and Scott Besson believe) is that you can grow money supply pretty quickly and keep rates relatively low, and consumer inflation comes down because of productivity gains. Businesses can set up production in cheaper locations, and now AI may crush inflation further. You saw recent articles suggesting AI will materially reduce inflationary pressures.

That dynamic drives asset prices up because central banks can keep rates low. What could stop it is political backlash: a populist revolution, wealth taxes, or national-level policies that respond to inequality. The criticism of both Trump and Biden is similar: "It’s not working for 60% of the people." Stocks and macro indicators can be strong, but many people still feel it’s harder to have the same life as before.

## The Fed, private sector, and AI's role
Interviewer: It's not lost on me that the private sector crushed inflation, not the Fed. How does that change monetary policy, especially with potential deregulation, tax cuts, tariffs, and AI?

Mike: All Americans should be relieved that Jay Powell is in his seat. Kevin Warsh (referred to earlier) took tail risk away — he's dovish and likely to cut rates the first time, and he believes rates should be a lot lower because of the AI piece. But if facts change, he'll change. He's a man of integrity and persuasive. Having someone steady like that helps the dollar stay important longer and be a good steward of the economy.

## Market structure and regulation: politics matter
Mike: Market structure could pass and would be supportive for crypto. I got a call from Chuck Schumer three nights ago — "Damn it, we're going to get this thing passed." I'm talking to both left and right. Both sides want to pass market structure for different reasons:

- The right sees it as a signature policy issue — "I'm the crypto president" — and wants to deliver.
- The left realizes crypto is popular, especially with younger voters, and wants to depoliticize the issue.

One outstanding issue is whether stablecoins should be able to give rewards that mimic interest. Banks say absolutely not; Brian Armstrong says absolutely yes. Intellectual arguments are in favor of interest-bearing stablecoins as consumer innovation, but it hurts banks. Senators have been telling industry to figure out a compromise, which frustrates Mike — that's the senators' job: set rules and be willing to upset some interests.

Mike thinks the bill will pass, and if it does it could create fresh narrative and flows into the space. Wall Street will participate because it sells products — once Wall Street joins, a 2% allocation to Bitcoin could become standard.

## Why market structure matters and tokenization potential
Mike: Wall Street is one of the greatest selling machines ever built. Once it sells crypto, that legitimizes the space. Market structure brings Wall Street in; Wall Street will sell tokenized assets that run on blockchains like Ethereum or Solana. Tokenizing assets (Galaxy stock or SpaceX shares) opens sales to billions of people outside the U.S. who lack great financial services — in places like Cambodia, Benin, Paraguay, and Uruguay. That's where crypto really matters: serving the underserved.

Paul Atkins said, "I want everything onchain." Once people have a wallet, they'll want some Bitcoin. Wall Street's participation helps accelerate adoption.

## Galaxy: crypto and AI businesses
Interviewer: You guys now are a crypto company and an AI company?

Mike: My soul is in crypto, but my wallet is in AI these days. We have 1.63 gigawatts of power that we own at Galaxy. Power is the scarce commodity and tenants (Microsoft, Nvidia, Amazon, Google) are racing to get power. It's really about power in 2027–2030, which we have. We leased out 800 megawatts and subleased to tenants.

Morgan Stanley said the data center assets may be worth double what our stock is trading at. Our crypto business did $500 million of revenue last year (the business itself, not the balance sheet). The balance sheet lost money because crypto was down.

We haven't given up on Bitcoin. There are 300 million people who own some Bitcoin; they're not going to say, "That was a mistake." We're going to have seller exhaustion, which I think we may already have.

Interviewer: Are you going to break up Galaxy into AI and crypto businesses?

Mike: We constantly evaluate what makes sense. It's not as easy as snapping your fingers. I'm looking two years ahead at capital structure and potential free cash flow — a data-center business could generate significant free cash flow that helps win in crypto. We'll take it quarter by quarter and make the right decision when it makes sense. One transaction can change thinking.

## Tokenized stock
Mike: We tokenized Galaxy — we were the first SEC tokenized stock. The bad news is it currently trades on Superstate, which is a closed garden with limited volume; you effectively need an appointment to trade it. We're working with the SEC; the SEC really wants tokenization to happen. One negotiation in the bill is sandbox authority for tokens to trade on decentralized platforms. Imagine a tokenized stock trading on Uniswap or Hyperliquid — that's the future of DeFi.

Is that fair to TradeFi firms with massive compliance infrastructures? Maybe not, but that's where innovation happens.

## Prediction markets and DeFi
Mike: Prediction markets are going to be huge. We missed a business angle there. Prediction markets can capture attention and liquidity quickly. One example went from zero to $10 billion in about a week because they allowed political trading and people followed attention. They now have 80% of revenue from sports betting. FanDuel and others are reacting — either join them or try to compete.

The CFTC made a ruling on parts of this and may tweak it. There's punch-counterpunch in regulation, and DeFi will see similar back-and-forth. Tokenization and DeFi will enable trading assets globally on distributed exchanges.

## Fantasy football anecdote
Interviewer: Tell us about the fantasy football league.

Mike: We've been playing this fantasy football league for 25 years. There's a replica of the Super Bowl trophy with a cricket on top — "the hopper." The story: first year, my partner Adam Levenson and I had amazing semifinals and I said, "Looks like it's going to be an all-fortress final," and then two five-standard-deviation events happened. Joe Horn had four touchdowns and we both lost. Someone told the story "don't jinx yourself" and called it "calling the hopper," so our trophy has a gold cricket on top.

The great thing about macro investors is we learn and share ideas. We're co-competitors in many ways, and many in that group have Bitcoin in their arsenal. Crypto is now part of dashboards like Bloomberg; everyone sees the S&P and the Bitcoin price. If people in the business think they can make money in crypto, they'll pour in. Right now, many of those guys are on the sidelines.
