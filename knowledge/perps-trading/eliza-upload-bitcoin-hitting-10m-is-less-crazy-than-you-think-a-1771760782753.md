## Self‑Belief, Founders, and the Silicon Valley Belief System

Key ideas:
- Self‑belief is a primary fuel for founders. Many talented people exist, but internalizing that you can do great things is often the bottleneck.
- Silicon Valley's mimetic belief system (seeing peers succeed) unlocks belief and agency in others.
- Some people are predisposed (genetics or upbringing), but many more high‑potential people are simply unidentified due to lack of access or opportunity.
- Crypto and the internet help surface global talent by providing capital and information more broadly.

Quote:
> "The real bottleneck for people to go do these amazing things is internalizing that they can do them."

## Plastic Surgery, Social Media, and Camera Distortion

- Avichal offers a theory: plastic surgery is evolving to optimize how people look in online photos (camera distortion), not necessarily how they look in person.
- This creates a "style drift" because people optimize for what looks best on camera (Instagram, iPhone).
- Result: different proportions and emphasis emerge in celebrities and influencers optimized for online impressions.

## From Founder to Investor: Realizing a Strength

- Avichal started as an entrepreneur (exit to Facebook), then began angel investing around 2016 after having capital from the exit.
- Early angel investments (Notion, Deel, Boom, Cruise, etc.) were at ~$10–15M caps; later many returned 100–1,000x.
- He realized he might be better at finding and supporting founders than starting companies himself.
- He reframed his role as more like a music producer (e.g., Rick Rubin) than a public markets trader: helping artists (founders) express authentic visions.

Quote:

## How Avichal Evaluates Founders

- Early (pre‑seed) investing: roughly 80% of the decision is about the person — their uniqueness, compulsions, worldview, and whether they are the person who can authentically express and execute the idea.
- Questions Avichal asks: Where were you born? What did your parents do? Why did you study that? What experiences gave you the obsession?
- He looks for authenticity, obsession, and an "X factor" (an intuitive sense that the person will persist and perform).
- Later stages (Series A+): more emphasis on product/market fit, go‑to‑market strategy, and other business mechanics.

## Missionaries vs Mercenaries: Long‑Term Mindset

- Missionaries believe in a mission even when economics aren't immediately compelling; they have staying power and survive volatility.
- Mercenaries chase short‑term price moves and are likely to get washed out.
- In volatile industries that trend "up and to the right," survival and long‑term persistence capture the back end of exponential growth.

Quote:
> "When you're a missionary, you're not doing it because of the number. You're doing it because you believe in the thing."

## Cognitive Biases That Make Crypto Hard to Intuitively Grasp

Three main biases:
1. Humans are not wired for exponential growth — primate biology doesn't experience exponential scale.
2. Poor intuition for large numbers — we can't easily reason about millions, billions, or global user bases.
3. Bad intuition for low‑probability / tail events — we misestimate low‑probability outcomes and loss aversion skews decision making.

Combined, these biases make early‑stage crypto (exponential growth + low probability + massive upside) extremely counterintuitive.

## Crypto Thesis: Why Crypto Matters

- Cryptography is a leveling, defensive technology: high‑quality cryptography is available to everyone (individuals get similar protections to large states).
- Crypto lets us reimagine key systems that have failed or become centralized: banks, voting, capital allocation, healthcare data, and data ownership.
- Decentralized tech plus global capital/information access helps identify and bootstrap talent worldwide (e.g., teams in Southeast Asia creating strong DeFi and protocol businesses).

## Bitcoin Valuation Reasoning (How high can Bitcoin go?)

Avichal's framework (not financial advice):
- Macro backdrop: unsustainable fiscal spending in Western countries. Options for governments:
  1. Cut entitlement spending (politically infeasible)
  2. Raise taxes (politically infeasible)
  3. Inflate the currency (debasing) — the path most governments favor
  4. Grow productivity enough to cover spending (possible but slow)
- In an inflationary/currency‑debasement regime, fixed‑supply, seizure‑resistant stores of value become attractive (gold, rare art, Bitcoin).
- Compare Bitcoin to gold:
  - If Bitcoin captures some of gold's market share, simple market‑cap math (gold market cap ÷ 21M BTC) yields very large per‑BTC price targets.
  - Back‑of‑envelope: Bitcoin at least in the high hundreds of thousands to low millions per BTC if it reaches the same market cap as gold. With additional TAM expansion from mobile distribution, Avichal says $5–10M per BTC in the fullness of time is not crazy.
- TAM expansion example: mobile apps and software historically expand market sizes (Uber example: taxi market turned into a much bigger market once reimagined on mobile).
- Important caveats:
  - Not a short‑term prediction; this is a decades‑scale view.
  - Prices depend on denominator effects (lost/locked BTC), distribution, and adoption.

Quote:
> "We're 15 years into Bitcoin. We're 10 years into ETH. If Bitcoin can be as big as gold, once you put it on a phone, once you put it in front of 5 billion people, the market size actually goes up by 5 to 10x."

## Ethereum, Solana, Sui — How Avichal Thinks About Other Chains

ETH:
- Avichal views ETH as also a type of store of value and a programmable economic layer.
- ETH may capture some of the same allocation that Bitcoin gets; rational investors may allocate across BTC and ETH for diversification.
- Institutional understanding of ETH is still maturing; ETH could repeat Bitcoin's institutional adoption curve over the next 5 years.

Solana:
- Avichal sees Solana as a "computational layer" with advantages for consumer/ social / financial experiences due to its fast, cheap architecture.
- Solana is well suited to end‑user, social, and entertainment experiments where dev velocity and low transaction cost matter.

Sui:
- Avichal and Electrical Capital are investors/partners in Sui.
- Sui is built by ex‑Facebook engineers; it uses Move as a VM/ programming language and is designed from lessons learned (e.g., Libra).
- Thesis: a purpose‑driven programming language and VM, created by experienced teams, can be the basis for a fast, modern L1 that spawns new use cases (e.g., distributed file systems like Walrus built on top).
- Avichal expects more L1s and infrastructure innovation; better performance/cost can unlock new classes of applications (machine‑to‑machine, sensors, etc.).

On the broader L1 landscape:
- Not expecting the market to settle on just a few chains — likely many hundreds of interesting and successful chains will coexist and specialize.

## Investing & Exit Thoughts

- Avichal believes much of the real compounding of winners happens in the "back 10–15 years" of a successful company or protocol, not the first 5–10 years.
- Investing in early founders is like betting on artists: you find authentic, obsessive people and help them express unique ideas into products and companies.
- Early investors should be comfortable being outsiders; mainstream attention often signals the top of a bull market and return compression.

## Closing Notes and Advice

- Avichal reiterates patience, long‑term thinking, and awareness of cognitive biases.
- Missionaries (long‑term believers) have staying power and are more likely to capture the compounding upside.
- Practical advice: resist short‑term temptations, be patient, and remember that many technologies and companies only realize their massive returns in the latter stages of long timelines.

Final quote from the conversation:
> "When you survive long enough, often the outcomes are actually quite significant."
