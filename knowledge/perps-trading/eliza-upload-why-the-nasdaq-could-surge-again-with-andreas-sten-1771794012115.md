## Capex, AI and the Earnings Outlook

Andreas frames the central question many fund managers are asking: is capex a good or bad thing? Many sell-side shops call the current capex cycle overinvestment and compare it to the run-ups before 2000 and the 1970s oil crises. Andreas disagrees.

Key points:
- Comparing current earnings/returns to the dotcom bubble or oil crises is misleading. The current earnings cycle does not match the extreme outsized returns of 2000.
- Andreas thinks markets are in an "AI fear regime" that is not fundamentally backed by return-on-investment prospects.
- Major tech companies (the “big four” — Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft, Apple implied) should be able to pay back capex investments in very few years given backlogs and expected returns.
- A large portion of capex is not heavily debt financed. Andreas estimates roughly $1.5 trillion of cumulative AI and data center expenditures since 2023, with less than $300 billion being debt financed — very different from the credit-fueled 2000 cycle.

Quote:
> "We're stuck in an AI fear regime right now, which is not really fundamentally backed."

Ral agrees that shifting capital from buybacks to capex (AI) is sensible if the investment will outperform share repurchases.

Andreas argues that even if a vendor like OpenAI fails, others (Amazon, Apple, Microsoft, Google) would absorb compute and accelerate the trend — not slow it.

## Manufacturing, Inflation, and the Goldilocks Setup

- Current live data suggests the manufacturing cycle is picking up; Andreas expects ISM manufacturing could reach the mid-50s within a couple of months.
- Capex among big tech is beginning to spill over into domestic capex.
- Inflation data from nowcasting shows low monthly inflation (15–17 basis points/month annualized), effectively below 2% when annualized.
- This combination — rising manufacturing/capex with subdued inflation — is a textbook Goldilocks scenario, not widely appreciated because markets are focused on AI fear narratives.

Andreas notes the role of credit analysts and skeptics: their job is to be cautious, and tech executives must demonstrate ROI over the next 1–2 years to convince them.

---

## Adoption, Implementation, and How Early We Are

- Many blue-chip companies are still a couple of years behind smaller, more agile firms and small-business owners in implementing AI.
- Examples:
  - An M&A shop limited to using Microsoft Copilot in an offline mode due to data sharing concerns, which made the tool less useful.
  - Large hedge funds and family offices where many teams still do not use AI tools despite availability.
- Andreas draws the parallel to email adoption in the late 1990s: adoption starts slowly, then rapidly becomes ubiquitous.
- If Netscape/Internet year-zero and ChatGPT year-zero analogy holds, we may be 3–5 years from a peak adoption phase.

Quote:
> "As soon as you recognize that we're still so early in the implementation of this, all of the talk about whether we're in a bubble is silenced."

## The S-curve of LLMs and Sector Drawdowns

- LLM capabilities appear to roughly double every 4–5 months in certain metrics; the slope is hard to predict, making extrapolation dangerous.
- Investors often over-extrapolate left-tail risks when faced with fast changes; sectors can suffer large drawdowns on a single negative narrative or white paper.
- Andreas observed many stocks experiencing >7% drawdowns intraday without a complete index crash — indicative of rolling, sector-by-sector repricing as market participants try to separate signal from noise.

## Emerging Markets and the AI Link

- Even the emerging markets trade is heavily AI-driven: large EM indices are concentrated in Korea and Taiwan because of semiconductor exports (TSMC, Samsung, Unix).
- Investors who fear capex are still buying the beneficiaries of capex (chip manufacturers, data center landlords), showing they expect capex to continue — they just fear the payers not receivers.

## The Barbell: Intelligence vs. Energy/Inputs

- Andreas and Ral describe a barbell:
  - One side: intelligence (AI, software, automation) — rising productivity and margins.
  - Other side: energy and inputs (solar, minerals, commodities) — the physical side that enables scale and must catch up.
- China is leading in energy buildout and grid/solar expansion; the US leads in intelligence and starts from a stronger oil & gas base. Both sides matter.

## Globalization, Labor, and a New Hyper-Globalized Labor Market?

- Ray Dalio’s "new world order" and deglobalization themes were discussed. Andreas highlights a twist:
  - Autonomous vehicle vendors (Alphabet’s AV unit) admitted to having remote operators in the Philippines to take over in emergencies. Tesla’s Optimus also reportedly used workers in India.
  - This suggests a potential for a hyper-globalized labor market enabled by AI, robotics, and VR — allowing labor to be sourced anywhere for remote control and training tasks.
- Labeling data and content moderation work in Africa and South/Southeast Asia is already feeding AI training jobs.

Quote:
> "You could probably end up hiring anywhere in the world with this combination of AI, robotics and virtual reality."

## Labor, Margins and the Economics of Automation

- Tech companies are harvesting AI returns with shrinking labor forces and rising earnings; margins should expand as high human costs get replaced by capex amortized over time.
- Example: AI-driven call centers and 24/7 autonomous services imply effectively limitless scale and margin upside if scaled properly.
- Historically, margins have trended up since 2000 and can go further with automation.

## Business Cycle and Fiscal Incentives

- Bonus depreciation reintroduced in the recent bill incentivizes doing capex now rather than later. This is a game changer that encourages capex across company sizes.
- Early signs of capex spending show up in quarterly reports at Alphabet and Amazon.
- Commodities (oil & industrials) are starting to benefit — energy gains since New Year have rewarded some energy investors and may continue through the year.

Downside risk:
- If energy-driven inflation passes through to the broader basket (likely 3–4 quarters out), that may slow investment rate of change and pose a risk.
- Andreas’s base case: for now, commodities and technology rising together is fine. The danger is technology rolling over while commodities continue — that’s when to be cautious.

## Monetary Policy, Productivity and the Greenspan Playbook

- Andy suggests a Greenspan-like playbook for the current cycle: government wants supply-side productivity gains and the Fed may "do nothing" in response, letting productivity lower inflation while GDP and corporate earnings expand.
- If the Fed (Kevin Walsh) cuts rates and sits back, the combination of fiscal support and productivity gains could allow the economy to run hot without rekindling high inflation.
- Andreas speculates we may never see another rate rise for a long time, given deflationary pressures from automation and rising productivity.

## Liquidity — Narrow vs. Broad

Andreas distinguishes between:
- Narrow liquidity: interbank reserves and Federal Reserve plumbing.
- Broader liquidity: deposits, corporate cash, bank credit creation.

Narrow liquidity:
- Still slightly tight. Recent "shutdowns" in narrow liquidity caused market hiccups.
- Fed has been purchasing T-bills via reserve management to avoid these hiccups — ramping up to around $20B per week (80–100B/month trajectory).
- Andreas expects these purchases to continue through the year to avoid market stress ahead of the midterms. 2027 may be different.

Broader liquidity:
- A smaller Fed balance sheet can only be sustained if the private sector (bank lending) expands first.
- ESLR changes and fiscal rebates (tax rebates and fiscal receipts) will push deposits back into banks, enabling leverage and credit creation.
- The TGA drawdown and fiscal rebates could provide a large deposit inflow into banks, enabling credit multipliers.

Market implications:
- Central banks globally are, on average, cutting rates and adding liquidity, the opposite of typical late-cycle behavior (1999/2007).
- This is supportive for markets and liquidity overall.

## The Basis Trade and Repo Funding Concerns

- Common leveraged hedge fund trades (buy Treasuries and asset-swap them) rely on stable funding markets.
- Growth in SOFR-based funding and asset swap trades means stable repo markets are important; spikes in SOFR increase funding costs and spill into treasuries and equities.
- Policymakers (including Scott Bessant) are aware and likely will support stable funding to avoid market hiccups.

---

## Where Will the Money Come From?

- Despite fund manager surveys showing high cash deployment (many are "all in"), liquidity for the market can come from:
  - Average central bank easing and liquidity injections globally.
  - Fiscal rebates and deposit inflows.
  - Expansion of commercial banking balance sheets and credit creation.
- The current macro setup (easing liquidity) is supportive for markets.

## Final Thoughts

- The current environment: positive liquidity, rising capex, domestic manufacturing pickup, and low inflation readings all combine to create a Goldilocks setup.
- Markets are confused by fast-paced AI narratives, leading to rolling drawdowns that may rebound once ROI from capex becomes clearer over the next few quarters.
- The cycle may lead to broad equity outperformance through 2027 and potentially a larger bubble phase thereafter if adoption and productivity accelerates.
- Ral and Andreas close the conversation optimistic about the capex cycle and the prospects for a Nasdaq rebound as the business cycle and AI-driven productivity play out.
